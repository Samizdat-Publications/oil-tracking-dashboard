# Design Brief — "Administrations: An Economic Comparison"

**Date:** 2026-08-02 · **Supersedes** the framing in `2026-08-02-v3-the-receipt.md`
(that brief's component-level guidance still applies; this one changes the concept,
the tone, and the above-the-fold).
**For:** Claude Design · **From:** Claude Code (implementing in `oil-dashboard/frontend`)

---

## 1. What changed and why

The previous framing ("Why your bills went up") was too editorial — it reads like an
opinion piece, which invites the reader to argue with the *author* instead of the *data*.

**New positioning: a nonpartisan-looking analytical instrument.** A comparison tool for
US economic performance across presidential administrations. The reader operates it and
reaches the conclusion themselves.

The conclusion doesn't change. The posture does. A tool that *looks* like it was built to
prove a point gets dismissed by exactly the audience it needs to reach; a tool that looks
like a Bloomberg terminal gets argued with on the numbers — which is the argument we win.

Working title: **"Administrations — An Economic Comparison"** or similar. Not a headline,
a *label*. It should read like the name of a dataset.

## 2. Audience

A skeptical reader on a phone, arriving from a Facebook link, who believes the current
economy is strong and the previous administration caused inflation. They are not hostile
to data — they have never been shown any. If the page pattern-matches to "political
content" in the first two seconds, they leave.

## 3. Above the fold — the single most important design problem

**Everything essential must land without scrolling, on a 375px phone.** Right now the page
opens with a form. That's wrong. It should open with the chart that tells the most story.

**The candidate for the hero chart** — my recommendation, but argue with it if you have
better:

> **A single continuous timeline, Clinton → today, of one headline metric, with the
> background banded by administration and colored by party.** The reader instantly sees:
> the metric, the party in power at every moment, and where the breaks are. One glance,
> no explanation needed.

The metric should be **switchable** right there in the hero — inflation, job creation,
real wages, gas prices, GDP. Changing it re-draws the same banded timeline. That single
interaction *is* the product: it says "pick any measure you like, here's the record."

Constraints on the hero:
- Legible at **375×667** without scrolling. That is roughly 240px of chart plus a title,
  a metric switcher, and one line of takeaway.
- Party bands must be readable without relying on color alone (accessibility, and ~8% of
  men are red-green colorblind — a red/blue political chart is the worst possible case).
  Use labels, texture, or boundary markers in addition to hue.
- No animation that delays comprehension. Draw-on is fine; a 3-second reveal is not.

## 4. Visual direction: American, restrained

**American flag palette — but institutional, not campaign.** The reference points are
Federal Reserve publications, Census Bureau data products, and the Statistical Abstract of
the United States. Think *government document*, not *rally*.

- Red and blue must be **exactly balanced in visual weight**. If the red is louder than
  the blue, the page reads as partisan and the argument dies. Test this literally: squint
  at it; neither side should dominate.
- Suggested restraint: navy and a muted brick red rather than saturated primaries; cream
  or off-white rather than pure white; a single gold/parchment accent if needed.
- The existing V3 dark theme can go if a light, document-like treatment serves better.
  **Your call.** Light probably reads as more "official" and prints/screenshots better,
  which matters for sharing.
- Charts are the design. Chrome should be nearly invisible.

⚠️ **Color assignment is load-bearing.** Red = Republican, blue = Democratic is the US
convention and must be followed. But it means every chart is pre-loaded with political
meaning, so the *neutral* elements (axes, gridlines, annotations) must be scrupulously
non-partisan in color — grey/black only.

## 5. The comparison engine (the centerpiece, placed late)

An interactive panel where the reader picks a metric and sees **every administration from
Clinton to today** ranked or plotted side by side, color-coded by party.

Metrics to support: headline inflation, core inflation, job creation per month, real
wages, unemployment, GDP growth, gas prices, oil prices, deficit, S&P 500.

Design problems to solve:
- **Terms are different lengths.** Trump's second term is ~19 months against 48-month
  terms. Everything must be rate-normalized (annualized), and the design should make that
  visible rather than hiding it — an in-progress term needs to *look* in-progress.
- **Aggregating by party is the reader's move, not ours.** Offer a "group by party" toggle,
  but default to showing individual administrations. Pre-aggregating looks like
  cherry-picking; letting them press the button themselves is persuasive.
- **Some metrics favor Republicans.** The tool must handle that gracefully and without
  editorializing — that's the entire credibility mechanism. If S&P 500 growth ranks Trump
  first, it shows that plainly.
- Honest attribution caveat, always visible: presidents don't control the economy on a
  four-year clock. Recessions and shocks are inherited. The design needs a persistent,
  non-defensive way to say this without undermining the whole exercise.

## 6. Chart inventory needed

Lots of charts, all with party-banded time axes:

| Chart | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Hero: banded timeline, metric-switchable** | The whole story in one view |
| **Paired bars: staples by term** | Ground beef, coffee, gas etc. — annualized, both terms |
| **Dollar cards** | "$5.55 → $6.83" — actual BLS prices, no index points |
| **Monthly job-creation columns** | Party-banded, zero-anchored; the 321k → 42k collapse |
| **Breadth chart** | Headline vs core vs median vs trimmed CPI, with 2% reference |
| **Administration ranking** | Sortable, groupable by party, one row per admin |
| **Small multiples** | Every metric at once, same banded x-axis, for scanning |
| **Real wages** | The one that matters most to a household |

## 7. Mobile is not a port

Assume **most** readers are on a phone in the Facebook in-app browser. Charts must be
designed mobile-first and *then* expanded, not shrunk. Specifically:
- Time-series charts on a 375px screen need fewer series, bigger strokes, and labels that
  don't collide.
- The comparison engine likely needs a fundamentally different layout on mobile (stacked
  cards?) rather than a scaled-down table.
- Touch targets ≥44px. Sliders and toggles must be usable with a thumb.
- Older readers: generous type (17px+ body), real contrast ratios.

## 8. Tone of copy

Neutral, declarative, specific. Let numbers carry emphasis instead of adjectives.

Good: *"Job creation averaged 321,000 per month, then 42,000."*
Bad: *"Job creation didn't slow down. It stopped."* (that's the current copy — too pleased
with itself)

Name people plainly and without loading: "Biden," "Trump," dates. No epithets, no scare
quotes, no "so-called."

## 9. Non-negotiables

- **Zero fabrication.** Every number from an endpoint or a labeled assumption. Missing data
  says "no data" — never a plausible-looking placeholder.
- **Every claim carries its falsifier**, rendered inline and expandable.
- **Rows that cut against the thesis stay visible.** Eggs are down 45%/yr — because avian
  influenza resolved, not because of policy, and the page says exactly that. The S&P is up.
  Initial claims are low. Showing these is why the rest is believable.

## 10. Constraints

React 19 + Vite + Tailwind v4 · hand-rolled SVG charts (Plotly is dropped) · CSS/rAF
animation, no animation library · `prefers-reduced-motion` respected · self-contained,
no external asset hosts.

## 11. Also needed: the OG share image

**1200×630 static PNG.** Facebook doesn't run JS, so this image *is* the link preview and
determines whether anyone clicks. It should carry one number and read at thumbnail size.
Same institutional-American treatment.
